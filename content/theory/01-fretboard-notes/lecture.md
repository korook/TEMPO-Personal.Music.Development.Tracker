# Notes on the fretboard

Knowing where the notes are turns the neck from a grid of numbers into a map. It is what lets you move a chord shape to a new key, find a root note fast, or talk about music with other players. You don't need to learn the whole neck at once. Start with two strings.

## The musical alphabet

Music uses seven letters: **A B C D E F G**. After G it starts again at A, one octave higher. Every note on the guitar has one of these names, sometimes with a sharp (♯) or flat (♭) added.

## Half steps and whole steps

On the guitar, moving up **one fret** raises the note by a **half step**. Two frets is a **whole step**.

Most letters are a whole step apart, so there is a note in between them. There are two exceptions, and they are worth remembering:

- **B to C** is only a half step (one fret).
- **E to F** is only a half step (one fret).

> A quick way to remember it: B and C, E and F sit right next to each other. Every other pair of letters has a gap of two frets.

## Sharps and flats

The notes in the gaps get two names. The note between F and G is **F♯** (F raised) or **G♭** (G lowered). Both names point to the same fret. Which name you use depends on the key, and that comes later in the course. For now, either is right.

## The open strings

From the thickest string to the thinnest, the open strings are **E A D G B E**. The two E strings are the same note, two octaves apart. In Tempo the thin one is written as a small **e**.

> Many guitarists remember them with a sentence: **E**ddie **A**te **D**ynamite, **G**ood **B**ye **E**ddie.

## Walking up the low E string

Start on the open low E and follow the alphabet, using the half step rule. E to F is one fret. F to G is two. G to A is two. A to B is two. B to C is one. C to D is two. D to E is two.

::neck strings=E notes=E,F,G,A,B,C,D frets=0-12 caption="The natural notes on the low E string"

At fret 12 you are back on E, one octave up. The double dot on most guitars marks that spot. From fret 12 the whole pattern repeats.

## The A string

The A string works the same way, starting from A:

::neck strings=A notes=A,B,C,D,E,F,G frets=0-12 caption="The natural notes on the A string"

These two strings matter most, because the root notes of most barre chords and power chords sit on them. If you know E and A, you can play an F, a B♭ or a C♯ chord without looking it up.

## Landmarks

You don't have to count from the nut every time. Use the dots:

- **Fret 3:** G on the low E string, C on the A string.
- **Fret 5:** A on the low E string, D on the A string.
- **Fret 7:** B on the low E string, E on the A string.
- **Fret 12:** the open-string notes again.

From a landmark, count one or two frets up or down. That is much faster than counting from zero.

## Octaves: find the same note again

Every note on the low E or A string has a partner one octave higher, **two strings up and two frets higher**. G at fret 3 on the low E string is also at fret 5 on the D string.

::neck marks="E:3:G,D:5:G,A:3:C,G:5:C" frets=0-12 caption="Octave shapes from the low E and A strings"

This shape lets you check yourself: if you know one note, you know its octave. (When the shape crosses onto the B string, it moves one fret further. That comes back later with scales.)

## How to practise this

1. Pick one note, say C. Find it on the low E string and on the A string. Say its name out loud.
2. Find its octave two strings up.
3. Do the same with the next note in the alphabet.

Five minutes a day is enough. The exercises for this topic drill the same moves on a neck you can tap, and the quiz checks the ideas.
